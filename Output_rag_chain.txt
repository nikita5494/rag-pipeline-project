2. Automotive Safety Integrity Levels (ASIL)
The Automotive Safety Integrity Level (ASIL) is the central risk classification mechanism in ISO 26262. It is
determined during the Hazard Analysis and Risk Assessment (HARA) process and ranges from QM (Quality
Management, no specific safety requirements) to ASIL D (the highest integrity level). The ASIL quantifies the
safety risk of a hazardous event and drives the stringency of all subsequent safety requirements.

3. AUTOSAR Adaptive Platform Architecture
The Adaptive Platform (AP) was introduced in 2017 to address the requirements of high-performance computing
applications in vehicles — particularly autonomous driving, sensor fusion, AI/ML inference, and over-the-air
software updates. The AP is built on a POSIX-compliant OS and supports dynamic software loading and
service-oriented communication.

7.2 Automatic Emergency Braking (AEB)
AEB autonomously applies emergency braking to prevent or mitigate collisions. Failure modes include false
activations (nuisance braking that can cause rear-end collisions) and missed activations (failure to protect

against genuine hazards). AEB typically carries ASIL D requirements for forward collision cases at highway
speeds. The safety architecture must include: multi-sensor confirmation, plausibility checks against road
topology, suppression logic for infrastructure objects, and E2E protected actuation commands.
7.3 Lane Keeping Assist (LKA) and Lane Departure Warning (LDW)
LKA applies steering torque to keep the vehicle within lane boundaries. Safety requirements are typically ASIL
B–C. Key concerns: false activation causing sudden lane departure, failure during lane change, performance in
poor visibility. Safety mechanisms: lane marking confidence thresholds, driver torque override detection,
deactivation on turn signal.
